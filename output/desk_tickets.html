Reflection

Written after comparing the plan on the ticket tabs against the run that actually happened, and against the itemised ledger on the Cash tab. Every count below is read off audit_trail.json for this pass, not typed in. Every approval on this pass was clicked by a human in the dashboard, one at a time.

How the agents performed on each ticket

Ticket 102 — the tightest work on the board

2 agents, one delegation, 6 tool calls, and the cheapest run of the three. Stevie read the lease, checked cash, parked the approval and stopped. It avoided the structural trap in this ticket: rent is the one obligation in the shop with no row in invoices, so an agent that goes looking for one reports "nothing owed" and is exactly backwards. It also did not treat the $250 reserve as a reason to refuse rent — correct, since the reserve binds discretionary spend, not an obligation already incurred. Nothing wasted, nothing missed.

Ticket 101 — diagnosis and service

4 agents, 3 delegations, 11 tool calls. David found the real blocker: the only vendor who can reprint apparel will not ship while invoice 501 stands, so a one-unit shortfall worth $8 is gated behind $840. Patrick costed it exactly and parked the request. He reached the shortfall through the ticket payload rather than calling check_stock — the number was right, but the cheaper read was also the less deliberate one.

Alexis wrote to Tauhid Zaman. Draft #1 says size S is unavailable, promises no shipment, and commits only to following up — the correct message when the honest answer is "not yet". An earlier run closed this ticket in silence, and the difference between the two is the difference between a ticket that is closed and a customer who has been served.

Ticket 103 — the most work, and the only one the shop refused

32 tool calls and 7 delegations, roughly 5.3× ticket 102, and it ended declined rather than resolved.

That is the right answer. The shop can supply 8 of 20 hoodies; closing the gap needs a $264 restock against $160 of cash and a $250 reserve. Declining with a reason and a drafted explanation beats recording a resolution the shop cannot deliver, and the board shows the distinction rather than flattening it.

What it got right beyond that: the 12-unit shortfall and the $264 restock are exact, the margin floor was computed at $36.67 ($22 cost ÷ 0.6), and the customer's own $49.00 offer was checked against that floor rather than waved through — 55.1% margin, $980 for twenty. The purchase order was put to a human as approval #5, approved, and then not funded, leaving a draft row and an untouched balance. That is the affordability guard doing its job after a human had already said yes, which is a stronger demonstration than a tool refusing an agent.

What it got wrong is unchanged and now thoroughly evidenced. Patrick raised approval #4 to pay invoice 501 — a bill ticket 101 already had pending. That is three runs in a row. It spent one of the approver's clicks on a duplicate that pay_invoice was always going to refuse.

What would have been simpler as a single agent with tools

Approval #4 is a failure the multi-agent design caused, and it is now reproduced three times. Patrick working ticket 103 has no idea that Patrick working ticket 101 already parked a request against invoice 501 — they are different runs holding different contexts. One agent carrying one context would not have asked twice. The guardrail catches it every time — see the Cash tab, where approval #4 is recorded as approved and then refused — but the architecture creates the situation the guardrail has to catch, and on this pass it cost a human an actual click.

Ticket 103 shows the coordination cost directly. Alexis delegated to Patrick, then to David, then reported to Johnny, who delegated to Patrick again; after the human decided, the closing run re-engaged accounting and customer service a second time — 7 hops in total to reach a conclusion resting on three numbers ($49 clears the floor, 12 units short, $264 unaffordable). The trail even contains the architecture arguing with itself: Alexis tried to delegate back to the boss and the framework refused it to avoid a loop. That beat cost a model round-trip and produced nothing.

Ticket 102 did not need a team at all. boss → facilities → boss is a full extra model round-trip, re-sending a system prompt and a set of tool schemas, to produce an answer one agent holding get_lease, get_cash_position and request_approval could have given directly.

The usual defence of the split is safety — customer service cannot call pay_invoice because the tool is not on its list. That is weaker than it looks, because the real guards live in the MCP server, not in the prompts: no overdraft, no double payment and no payment without an approved approval are enforced in _spend_guard and _check_approval. This run proved it twice over — both the duplicate payment and the unaffordable purchase order were stopped after a human approved them. A single agent holding all seventeen tools would hit exactly the same walls.

What the split genuinely bought was role-specific prompts that encode role-specific traps — the size trap for inventory, "rent has no invoice row" for facilities, and the line that makes a customer draft a closing condition rather than a nicety — and a dashboard where the work is legible as a conversation. Both are real gains, and the second one is what fixed ticket 101. Neither required five separate model contexts; one longer prompt could carry the same warnings. The design would begin to pay for itself with hundreds of tickets worked in parallel, or with different roles needing different approvers or permissions. On a board of three, it cost more than it returned.

Three new problems this team could solve with the tools we built

  1. The Yale AI Club accepts the eight hoodies at $49.00
    Discount approval #3 is already granted at that price. check_stock confirms eight in size M, save_draft_message writes the confirmation and update_ticket reopens and closes it. No new tooling at all — and it would turn this board's one declined ticket into a partial sale.
  2. Elm City Gifts sends an invoice for mugs
    find_restock_vendor already matches on specialty, so "mugs and small goods" routes correctly rather than defaulting to the apparel printer. Patrick weighs it against the $160 remaining using the written payment priority, then request_approval and pay_invoice. If the amount exceeds $160 the tool refuses — which is the correct answer, not a failure.
  3. A customer wants the tee in size M
    Five in stock at $28 list. This is the happy path the board never exercised: check_stock reports it fillable, Alexis drafts the confirmation, the boss resolves. Worth noting only because every ticket we ran was a problem — the team has never actually completed an easy one.

Three it could not solve, and what each would need

  1. Receiving restocked goods
    No tool in the server writes inventory.qty. Purchase order 1 for twelve hoodies sits at draft and could never progress even if it were funded: the purchase_orders table declares a received status that no code path can reach.
    Needs: a receive_purchase_order tool that increments stock and closes the order, and an inventory agent permitted to call it. Without it the restock half of the shop is write-only.
  2. Any money coming in
    No tool increases cash_accounts.balance — the Cash tab only ever falls, by design. So the shop has approved a $49.00 price on ticket 103 that it has no mechanism to collect: selling the eight hoodies in stock would be $392.00 the ledger cannot record, and that $392 would have covered the $264 restock that forced the decline.
    Needs: a record_sale tool, a receivables concept so an unpaid customer order is distinct from cash, and plausibly a sales agent. It also means revisiting the "cash only goes out" rule, which is load-bearing in every affordability decision the team makes.
  3. Time passing
